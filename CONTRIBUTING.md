# Contributing to HCOS™ Human-Centered AI

Thank you for your interest in contributing to the HCOS™ Human-Centered AI framework.

HCOS is intended to remain a learning system.

Constructive disagreement, stress testing, practical examples, evidence, alternative interpretations, and identification of unintended consequences are therefore valuable contributions.

The purpose of contribution is not simply to expand the framework.

It is to make the framework:

**clearer, more accurate, more useful, more human-centered, and more accountable to reality.**

---

## Contribution Philosophy

HCOS begins with a stewardship principle:

> **A human-centered framework should remain open to evidence, correction, perspective, and learning.**

Contributors are encouraged to identify:

* assumptions that may be wrong,
* concepts that overlap unnecessarily,
* missing perspectives,
* edge cases,
* conflicting human needs,
* unintended consequences,
* power imbalances,
* implementation risks,
* unclear language,
* or situations where an HCOS principle produces an undesirable result.

A contribution does not need to agree with the current framework to be valuable.

Well-reasoned disagreement is welcome.

---

## What Contributions Are Welcome?

Useful contributions may include:

### Conceptual Refinements

Clarification or improvement of existing HCOS constructs.

### Stress Tests

Cases that challenge the framework or reveal where principles conflict.

### Applied Examples

Realistic applications in areas such as:

* healthcare,
* education,
* workplaces,
* government,
* technology,
* caregiving,
* accessibility,
* creative work,
* or community systems.

### Research and Evidence

Relevant research that supports, challenges, or refines an HCOS claim.

### Instruments

Improvements to assessment questions, implementation tools, or decision pathways.

### Accessibility Improvements

Changes that make HCOS easier to understand or use across different abilities, literacy levels, professional backgrounds, or contexts.

### Language Improvements

Simplification, clarification, correction, or identification of unnecessary jargon.

### Failure Modes

New ways in which otherwise well-intended human-centered technology could fail.

---

## Contribution Standard

A proposed change should ideally answer:

**What problem does this change solve?**

**What evidence, reasoning, or lived experience supports it?**

**What humans or perspectives might be affected?**

**What new risk could the change introduce?**

**How does it relate to existing HCOS constructs?**

**Could the same goal be achieved more simply?**

The framework should not grow simply because another concept can be named.

> **Conceptual expansion should earn its complexity.**

---

## Human-Centered Contribution Test

Before submitting a significant change, consider:

**Dignity**
Does the proposal preserve the inherent worth of affected humans?

**Autonomy**
Does it preserve meaningful participation and appropriate authority?

**Truth**
Is the claim appropriately supported and open to correction?

**Perspective**
Whose perspective is represented, and whose may be missing?

**Power**
Who gains or loses authority under the proposed change?

**Capacity**
Does the proposal increase or unnecessarily reduce human capacity?

**Stewardship**
Are benefits, burdens, risks, and responsibilities being allocated appropriately?

**Human Outcome**
What is likely to happen to humans if the proposal is adopted?

## Stress Testing Is Encouraged

HCOS should be tested against difficult cases rather than protected from them.

Useful stress tests include situations involving:

* conflicting autonomy,
* uncertainty,
* emergency authority,
* vulnerable humans,
* children,
* disability,
* organizational power,
* AI-generated decisions,
* conflicting rights,
* scarce resources,
* unintended technological consequences,
* or situations where every available option creates some burden or harm.

A good stress test may reveal that HCOS needs revision.

That is valuable.

---

## Evidence and Uncertainty

When making factual or empirical claims:

* cite reliable sources where possible,
* distinguish evidence from interpretation,
* identify important uncertainty,
* avoid overstating conclusions,
* and identify conflicting evidence when relevant.

HCOS should not turn confidence into truth merely because a claim supports the framework.

---

## Lived Experience

Lived experience may contain important information that formal metrics or institutional records fail to capture.

It should be heard seriously.

It should not automatically be treated as conclusive evidence of every interpretation.

HCOS therefore seeks:

**Lived Experience + Evidence + Context + Perspective + Discernment**

rather than dismissing either experiential or empirical knowledge.

---

## Language

Prefer language that is:

* understandable,
* precise,
* respectful,
* non-dehumanizing,
* and accessible outside highly specialized disciplines.

Technical language may be appropriate when needed.

When a concept can be expressed accurately in simpler language, prefer the simpler language.

---

## AI-Assisted Contributions

AI tools may be used to support contributions.

If AI materially assists with:

* drafting,
* analysis,
* synthesis,
* research organization,
* or editing,

the human contributor remains responsible for reviewing the submission.

AI-generated output should not be treated as evidence merely because it is fluent or confident.

Human contributors remain responsible for:

* factual accuracy,
* citations,
* reasoning,
* intellectual property compliance,
* and the final submitted content.

---

## Respectful Disagreement

Critique ideas rather than people.

Do not assume disagreement reflects:

* lack of intelligence,
* bad intent,
* lack of compassion,
* resistance to technology,
* or moral failure.

Different humans may prioritize legitimate values differently.

The objective is not artificial agreement.

The objective is better understanding and wiser design.

---

## Pull Requests

For substantive changes, please explain:

### Problem

What issue are you addressing?

### Proposed Change

What should change?

### Reasoning

Why is the change needed?

### Human Impact

Who could benefit or be burdened?

### Tradeoffs

What may be gained or lost?

### Evidence

What supports the change?

### Relationship to HCOS

Which existing constructs or principles are affected?

Small corrections do not require extensive explanation.

---

## New Constructs

Before proposing a new named HCOS construct, consider whether the issue can already be addressed through an existing concept.

A new construct should ideally:

* identify a distinct phenomenon,
* solve a recurring problem,
* add practical explanatory value,
* remain understandable,
* and avoid unnecessary duplication.

HCOS should resist conceptual inflation.

---

## Naming and Attribution

Please do not present modified versions of HCOS materials as officially approved HCOS standards unless they have been incorporated into the canonical repository.

Forks, adaptations, commentary, and critique should clearly distinguish themselves from canonical HCOS materials.

Use of HCOS names, trademarks, or branding may be subject to separate intellectual-property terms.

---

## Governance

Maintainers may decline or revise contributions that:

* conflict with the core human-centered purpose,
* duplicate existing constructs without sufficient benefit,
* rely on unsupported claims,
* create unnecessary complexity,
* introduce dehumanizing assumptions,
* or materially reduce clarity without corresponding value.

Rejection of a contribution does not necessarily mean the underlying concern is invalid.

It may mean another approach is needed.

---

## Core Contribution Principle

> **Do not protect HCOS from criticism. Protect its ability to learn from criticism.**

The framework should remain capable of changing when reality demonstrates that its assumptions, language, architecture, or recommendations are incomplete.
